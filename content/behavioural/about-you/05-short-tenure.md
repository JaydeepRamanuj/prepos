## Question
"You've been at your current job for only about a year. Why are you moving so soon? How do we know you won't leave us in a year too?"

## Clues
- The real question is about risk: show the move is deliberate, not a pattern of restlessness. Point to the 1.5 years you stayed as a trainer.
- Show what you *got done* in the year, so it reads as "learned and delivered a lot" rather than "left early".
- End with a forward commitment: what would make you stay for years, and why this role has it.

## Interview Answer
That's a fair question. A year is short, so I want to be clear that this isn't a habit. I stayed a year and a half as a trainer and left only when I'd decided to move from teaching into building software. Joining [company] was the right first step. In this year I [key achievements, e.g. "shipped X and Y", "went from bug fixes to owning a module"], so I got real production experience quickly. What I've also learned is what I need next: [e.g. "a team with senior engineers to learn from, code review, and products at scale"], and realistically that isn't available where I am, because [neutral structural reason, e.g. "the team is small and focused on client delivery"]. So I'd rather make one considered move now than stay two more years without growing. That's also why I'm being selective. I'm looking for a place I can stay at for a long time, and [specific reasons about this company] are exactly what would keep me here.

## Explanation
### What the interviewer is really assessing
- **Flight risk.** Hiring costs months. They need to believe you will stay 2+ years.
- **Judgement.** Was the move thought through, or a reaction to one bad week?
- **Honesty under pressure.** A defensive or evasive tone is worse than the short tenure itself.

### Template
- **Situation** — Acknowledge the fact calmly: "~1 year, yes." Show the longer stint (1.5 years training) so there's no pattern.
- **Task** — What you set out to get from this job: [production experience, real codebase, shipping]. Show you got it.
- **Action** — What you achieved and what you tried before deciding to leave: [achievements], [asked for / volunteered for X].
- **Result** — The clear, structural gap ([mentorship / scale / ownership]), a deliberate decision, and the commitment to stay at the right place.

### Story ideas from your background
- The career switch from trainer to developer as evidence that you move with intent.
- Concrete outcomes from your first year that a 3-year employee would also be proud of.
- What you'd look for to stay long term (mentors, ownership, product scale), mapped to this company.

### Variants of the question
- "Your resume shows short stints. Should we be worried?"
- "What would it take for you to stay 3+ years somewhere?"
- "What will you do if you join and the work isn't what you expected?"

## Follow-ups
- **What if you get bored here in a year?** — Say what you'd do first: talk to your manager, ask for a bigger scope, take on initiatives. Leaving is the last option, not the first.
- **Did your current company not offer a promotion or raise?** — Answer factually; then bring it back to learning and scope, not money.
- **Is there anything else going on, a bad manager or layoffs?** — If there's a real external reason (layoffs, project shutdown), say it plainly. It's a neutral fact and usually helps.

## Common Mistakes
- Getting defensive or over-justifying for two minutes. Calm and brief beats long and nervous.
- Blaming the company ("they over-promised", "bad culture"), which shifts the worry to your attitude.
- Hiding the trainer period, which actually proves you stay when you are learning.
- Promising "I'll never leave". It isn't credible; describe the conditions under which you stay.

## Tips
- Acknowledge first ("fair question"), then answer. That alone removes most of the tension.
- Three moves: **no pattern → I delivered → deliberate, selective move**.
- Ask them at the end what growth looks like for people in this role after 2–3 years. It reinforces that you are thinking long term.
