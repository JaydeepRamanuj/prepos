## Question
"Why are you looking to leave your current company?" Close variants: "Why are you switching now?", "What's missing in your current role?"

## Interview Answer
I've had a good year at [company]. I went from [starting point, e.g. "fixing UI bugs"] to [current scope, e.g. "owning the dashboard module"], and I learned a lot about shipping production React and Next.js. The reason I'm looking now is growth. The team is [small / mostly delivery-focused / working on a single client project], so most of my work is [e.g. "building screens against fixed specs"], and there's limited scope to [design systems / own features end to end / work on performance or backend]. I tested how far I could stretch it: I [e.g. "volunteered for the API integration layer" / "proposed and set up code review guidelines"], and that's where I learned the most. I want that to be my normal work, not the exception. So I'm looking for a product company with strong engineers, real code review and problems at scale, and from what I've read about [this company, e.g. "how your team ships"], that's what I'd get here.

## Explanation
### What the interviewer is really assessing
- **Are you running away or moving towards something?** "Towards" candidates stay longer and perform better.
- **Will you badmouth us later?** If you criticise your current employer, they assume you'll criticise them too.
- **Is the reason something we actually fix?** If you leave for "more ownership" and their role is pure ticket work, you'll leave again. Your reason should match what this role offers.

### Template (not strictly STAR, but the same discipline)
- **Situation** — What has been good: [what you learned, how your scope grew in ~1 year].
- **Task / gap** — The honest, neutral limit: [type of work, team size, tech, mentorship, scale]. Describe the *work*, never the *people*.
- **Action** — What you did to close the gap inside the job first: [volunteered for X, side project Y, proposed Z]. This proves you are not a quitter.
- **Result / direction** — What you want next, and why this company provides it: [ownership, scale, engineering culture, backend exposure] → [specific thing about them].

### Story ideas from your background
- A moment you went beyond your tickets (performance fix, reusable component, tooling) and enjoyed it more than your usual work.
- Your side projects (Postgres, Redis, Firebase) as evidence that you are already investing in growth on your own time.
- The trainer → developer switch as a pattern: you move deliberately when you stop learning, not impulsively.

### Variants of the question
- "What would make you stay at your current company?"
- "What don't you like about your current job?"
- "Have you discussed this with your manager?"
- "Is this about money?"

## Follow-ups
- **Is it about salary?** — Be honest but not salary-first: "Compensation matters, and I expect a fair market correction, but I'd not move for money alone to a role where I'm not learning."
- **Did you try to get that growth internally?** — Yes: name what you asked for or volunteered for, and what the realistic limit was.
- **What if our role also has a lot of routine work?** — Every job has routine work; what matters is whether there is also ownership and feedback. Ask what a typical sprint looks like.

## Common Mistakes
- Blaming the manager, the team or "bad management". Even if true, it reads as a red flag.
- A vague answer like "looking for new challenges", which sounds rehearsed and says nothing.
- Giving a reason the new role does not solve (e.g. "I want to do more backend" for a pure-frontend role).
- Over-explaining. This should be 45–60 seconds, then stop.

## Tips
- Use the formula: **grateful → gap → tried → towards**. One sentence each.
- Research the company before the interview and end the answer with one concrete link to them (their stack, product, engineering blog, scale).
- Never say anything about your current employer you would not say in front of them.
