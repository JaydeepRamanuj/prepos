## Question
"Tell me about yourself." Sometimes phrased as "Walk me through your background" or "Introduce yourself in two minutes." It is almost always the first question, and it sets the frame for everything after it.

## Interview Answer
I'm a frontend engineer working mainly with React, Next.js and TypeScript. Right now I'm a React and Next.js developer at [company], where I [own / build] [the main product area, e.g. "the customer dashboard"]; the piece I'm proudest of is [one concrete outcome, e.g. "rebuilding the checkout flow, which cut drop-off by X%"]. Before this I spent about a year and a half as a technical trainer, teaching web development and React to [students, business owners and career switchers]. That job made me very good at explaining things clearly and at debugging other people's code fast, because I'd see [N] different broken versions of the same project every week. Moving into a product role was a deliberate choice: I wanted to build and ship real software, not only teach it. Over the last year I've also gone deeper on the backend side through [side project, e.g. "a full-stack app with Postgres and Redis"], and I use AI coding agents daily, with a review-first workflow. I'm now looking for a team where I can own features end to end and learn from strong engineers, which is why [this role / company] caught my attention.

## Explanation
### What the interviewer is really assessing
- **Can you tell a coherent story in 60–90 seconds?** This is a communication test before it is a content test.
- **Does your path make sense?** A trainer → developer move invites the silent question "is this person a real engineer?" Your answer should settle that before anyone asks it.
- **What should they ask next?** Whatever you mention becomes the menu for follow-up questions. Only mention things you want to be asked about.

### The structure: present → past → future
This is not a STAR story. Use a three-beat pitch:

| Beat | Time | Fill in |
|---|---|---|
| **Present** | ~30s | Current title, stack, what you own, one quantified win: [title] at [company], building [area] with [stack]. Proudest result: [outcome + number]. |
| **Past** | ~25s | Trainer years framed as an asset: [how many] learners, [what you taught], the skill it gave you (explaining, debugging, patience, structure). |
| **Future** | ~15s | Why you are looking, phrased as moving *towards* something: [ownership / scale / backend depth / engineering culture] → link to this company. |

If you have STAR-style detail on the win, hold it back. Let them ask.

### Story ideas from your background
- The single most impressive feature you shipped at your current job, with a metric (performance, conversion, bugs closed, time saved).
- A trainer moment that shows engineering, not just teaching: building the course projects yourself, creating a starter template, reviewing student code.
- A side project that proves backend curiosity: what it does, which stack (Postgres, Redis, Firebase), one interesting problem you solved.
- Your AI-agent workflow as a productivity point, framed as "I plan, the agent drafts, I review and test".

### Variants of the question
- "Walk me through your resume."
- "Tell me something that's not on your resume."
- "How did you get into software development?"
- "Give me a quick introduction and then tell me about your current project."

## Follow-ups
- **Why did you move from training to development?** — You wanted to build and own real products; training gave you strong fundamentals and communication, but you were missing production experience. Keep it forward-looking.
- **What exactly did you build at [company]?** — Have one feature ready in STAR form with your specific role, decisions and outcome.
- **Do you miss teaching?** — Say you still use it: onboarding teammates, writing docs, explaining decisions in PRs. That turns a doubt into a strength.

## Common Mistakes
- Reading out the resume in chronological order, starting with school. The interviewer has the resume; they want the story.
- Apologising for the trainer years ("I only did training…"). Frame them as 1.5 years of explaining and debugging web code every day.
- Going past two minutes. Long intros signal that your answers to everything else will also be long.
- Ending without a "why here" sentence, which leaves the story hanging.

## Tips
- Write it, cut it to ~180 words, then say it out loud with a timer until it is under 90 seconds and sounds natural, not memorised.
- Put one number in the present beat. "Reduced load time from 4s to 1.5s" is remembered; "worked on performance" is not.
- Prepare a 30-second version too, for when the interviewer says "just briefly".
