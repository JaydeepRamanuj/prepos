## Question
"Tell me about a time you helped a teammate or someone junior improve. What did you do, and what changed?"

## Interview Answer
As a trainer I mentored [N] learners, but the story I'd pick is [name a role, e.g. "a career switcher from a non-IT background"] who was close to quitting in [month / module]. They could follow tutorials but froze on a blank editor, especially with React state. My goal wasn't to fix their code. It was to get them to build their [capstone project] on their own. First I worked out where they actually got stuck: the issue wasn't React, it was breaking a problem into steps. So I changed my approach: in each session they'd write the plan in plain comments first, then code one comment at a time, and I'd only answer questions with a question. I also gave them smaller daily exercises instead of the big project. In [N] weeks they finished the capstone themselves, and [outcome, e.g. "got a job as a junior frontend developer" / "went on to help other learners"]. I've used the same approach at [company] when [teammate / intern] joined: [what you did, e.g. "pair sessions on our codebase and a short onboarding doc"], which cut their ramp-up to [time].

## Explanation
### What the interviewer is really assessing
- **Leadership without authority.** At 2–3 years you aren't a manager, but teams want people who make others better.
- **Diagnosis.** Did you find the *real* problem, or just give answers?
- **Empathy and patience**, and whether you can adapt your style to the person.

This is the question where your trainer background is a clear advantage. Most 2–3 year candidates have one weak "I helped an intern" story; you have dozens. Pick the one with the clearest transformation.

### STAR template
- **Situation** — Who they were and what was hard for them: [background], [specific struggle].
- **Task** — What success meant: [finish a project / be independent on X / pass an interview].
- **Action** — Diagnose → adjust → build independence: [how you found the root cause], [method you changed, e.g. pseudocode-first, pairing, smaller exercises], [how you made yourself unnecessary].
- **Result** — Measurable change: [completed X], [got a job], [ramp-up time], [what they said or did afterwards].

### Story ideas from your background
- A struggling learner who went on to get a developer job.
- Restructuring a module or exercise after seeing many learners fail at the same point (that's mentoring at scale).
- Onboarding a new joiner or intern at your current company.
- Code review comments you turned into a team guideline or doc.

### Variants of the question
- "How do you help a teammate who keeps making the same mistake?"
- "Tell me about a time you onboarded someone."
- "Describe a time you shared knowledge with your team."

## Follow-ups
- **What if they didn't want help?** — Offer, don't impose; create an easy entry point (pairing on their ticket) and respect their pace.
- **How do you balance mentoring with your own deliverables?** — Time-box it (fixed pairing slots), and make it reusable (docs, recorded walkthroughs).
- **What did you learn from mentoring?** — For example, that "explaining the answer" is slower than "asking the right question" in the short term, but much faster in the long term.

## Common Mistakes
- A story where you just fixed their bug. That's helping, not mentoring.
- No visible change in the other person. The result must be about *them*, not you.
- Only trainer stories, which leaves "but can you do it in an engineering team?" open. Add one work example, even a small one.

## Tips
- Use the word "independent". Good mentoring makes the person need you less.
- If asked about leadership aspirations, this story is your evidence.
- Keep the person anonymous ("a learner from a commerce background"). Never use real names.
