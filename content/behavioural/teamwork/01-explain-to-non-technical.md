## Question
"Tell me about a time you had to explain something technical to someone non-technical. How did you make sure they understood?"

## Interview Answer
When I was a trainer, one of my batches was [business owners] who wanted to understand enough web development to [manage their own website / work with an agency]. One of them, [role, e.g. "a shop owner"], kept asking why their site was "slow on phones but fine on the laptop". My task was to explain it in a way that helped them make a decision, not to give a lecture. I skipped the jargon and used an analogy: the page is like [a parcel], and right now the parcel is [5 MB] because the photos were uploaded at full camera size, so on a mobile network it's like carrying that parcel up the stairs instead of taking a lift. Then I showed them, live, the same page loading [with throttled network] before and after compressing one image. I checked understanding by asking them to explain back what they'd change. The result: they [resized and compressed their product photos / asked their agency for image optimisation], and the page went from [X s] to [Y s] on mobile. I use the same approach at work now, for example when I explained [a trade-off, e.g. "why a feature needed an extra sprint"] to our [PM / client].

## Explanation
### What the interviewer is really assessing
- **Can you adjust to your audience?** Engineers who can only talk to engineers create friction with PMs, designers, clients and leadership.
- **Do you check for understanding**, or just transmit and hope?
- **Do you connect the explanation to a decision?** Non-technical people need to know what to *do*, not how it works internally.

This is your strongest category. 1.5 years of training gives you more real stories here than most candidates at your level.

### STAR template
- **Situation** — Who was the person, what did they need to decide, why did the technical detail matter? [person / role], [context].
- **Task** — What did they need to understand, and by when? [concept], [decision it unlocked].
- **Action** — How you explained it: [analogy], [visual or live demo], [removed jargon], [checked understanding by asking them to explain back / asking what they would do next].
- **Result** — What they did with it, plus a measurable outcome if possible: [decision made], [metric], [feedback you received].

### Story ideas from your background
- Explaining frontend vs backend, or what an API is, to business-owner learners.
- Explaining to a career switcher why their code "works on my machine" but not after deploy (env vars, build step).
- At work: explaining to a PM/client why a "small UI change" touches several screens, or why caching made data look stale.
- Explaining SEO / server rendering to a marketing person.

### Variants of the question
- "How do you communicate technical risk to a product manager?"
- "Tell me about a time a stakeholder misunderstood a technical constraint."
- "Explain [concept, e.g. an API / caching] to me as if I'm not technical." (They may ask you to do it live.)

## Follow-ups
- **Explain what an API is to me like I'm a non-technical person, right now.** — Have a ready analogy: a waiter takes your order (request) to the kitchen (server) and brings back food (response); you never go into the kitchen.
- **How did you know they understood?** — Teach-back: they explained it in their own words, or made the right decision afterwards.
- **What if they still didn't get it?** — Change the medium: a drawing, a live demo, a smaller piece of the problem.

## Common Mistakes
- Picking a story where the "non-technical person" was a fellow developer.
- Describing the explanation but not the outcome: what changed after they understood?
- Sounding condescending about the other person's knowledge.

## Tips
- Name your method explicitly: **analogy → demo → teach-back**. It sounds deliberate and trained, because it is.
- Have one trainer story and one current-job story ready; use whichever fits the company better.
- Be ready to do it live. Prepare 2–3 analogies (API, caching, server vs client rendering).
